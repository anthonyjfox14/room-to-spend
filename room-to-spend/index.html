Each one buys the same life for about the same pay. Pick one to see it in full.

    Take someone in New York who takes home $100,000. About a fifth of their spending goes on housing, a sixth on healthcare, including what insurance pays, and under a tenth on eating out and hotels. In Lisbon, housing costs 54% less, healthcare 55% less and eating out 33% less. Groceries cost 3% more and getting around 17% more. Housing and healthcare are big parts of the budget, so their lower prices matter most. Add it all up and the same life in Lisbon costs $70,386.

    We assume you spend all of what you take home, and figures are for one person in 2026. Rent differs city by city. Most other prices are set country by country. In the United States and the United Kingdom every city's rent comes from official figures. In Australia, Belarus, Belgium, Brazil, Cambodia, Canada, Chile, China, Czechia, Egypt, France, Germany, Greece, India, Indonesia, Israel, Italy, Japan, Kazakhstan, Kenya, Malaysia, Mexico, Netherlands, Paraguay, Peru, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Korea, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand, Turkey and United Arab Emirates, official or published figures tell us how much dearer or cheaper each city they cover is than the country's other covered cities. Cities those sources leave out take the level of the cheapest covered city, or in Turkey the middle of the provinces the source publishes. In other high-income countries we estimate each city's rent from how rich and how big it is, a method we checked against those official figures. Elsewhere a country's cities share one figure. US metro areas, Japanese prefectures, seven Canadian cities and UK regions also have official prices for most other spending. We use them when both cities have them, and always within one country. The UK and Canadian figures are from 2016 and 2019, the last published, and we assume the differences between cities still hold. Prices for 2026 are projected from the World Bank's 2021 price survey. Under each answer we give the range it is likely to fall in four times out of five. The range is wider where we know less about a city's rent, its other prices or its country's prices, and the size of each of those gaps is partly our own judgement. Housing is hardest to compare in city-states and the Gulf, where much of it is public or provided by employers. A few countries without a full price breakdown, such as Russia, take one overall price level for everything. We leave out countries where conflict or the exchange rate means prices cannot be compared.
